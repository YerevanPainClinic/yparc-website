Healthy lifestyle and persistent pain

Small, sustainable changes can support sleep, confidence in movement and daily life with pain. They complement treatment rather than replace it.

Chronic pain persists or recurs for more than three months. Tissue health, the nervous system, sleep, emotions and surroundings can all influence it. This does not make pain imaginary or the patient’s fault. The aim is to restore everyday abilities as well as reduce pain. [6]

Sleep: part of pain management

Pain can disturb sleep, while poor sleep can contribute to worsening pain. Sleep is therefore part of pain assessment. [1]

Keep roughly consistent bedtimes and wake times. Wind down in the evening, limit late caffeine and keep the bedroom dark and quiet. Do not use alcohol as a sleep aid. Discuss persistent insomnia with your doctor. [2]

Movement: manageable and regular

For chronic low back pain, exercise can reduce pain and improve function. Responses vary, and no single programme suits everyone. [3]

Start with activity you can repeat, such as a short walk or exercises selected by a professional, then build gradually. Avoid doing everything on a good day and being inactive the next. Follow individual restrictions after injury or surgery.

Managing stress does not dismiss pain

Psychological pain care teaches ways to manage fear and daily difficulties. Cognitive behavioural therapy can produce small average improvements in pain, distress and disability. It is more than advice to think positively. [4]

Make time for calm breathing, an enjoyable activity or contact with people close to you. Seek professional support if fear or low mood interfere with recovery.

Food: sustainable habits without miracle claims

Balanced eating can support general health and pain care. Choose varied vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and suitable protein sources. No single food or supplement removes everyone’s pain. Discuss restrictive diets or high-dose supplements with a professional. [5]

Where to start this week

Choose just two realistic steps, such as a daily short walk and a calm evening routine. Track sleep, walking ability or daily tasks alongside pain. Easier movement can be progress even if pain has not completely gone.

When to seek medical advice

Seek assessment if pain persists, worsens or disrupts sleep and daily life. New marked weakness, bladder or bowel problems, a sudden severe headache, chest pain or breathing difficulty need urgent medical help.
